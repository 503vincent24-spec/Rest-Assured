Assembly Guide

Furniture Assembly Best Practices for Moving Day

By the Rest Assured Moving crew — Salem, Oregon

Almost every furniture assembly problem we get called to fix traces back to one of three moments: disassembly, transport, or reassembly. Here's where each one goes wrong — and the system that prevents all three.

The real problem: hardware management

The single most common furniture assembly failure isn't a tool problem or a skill problem — it's a hardware problem. Specifically: all the screws, bolts, cam locks, and dowels for an entire bedroom set end up in one zip-lock bag with no indication of which piece they came from.

Two weeks later, standing in front of a disassembled bed frame in a new house, you're holding a bag of 40 mixed fasteners with no memory of which size goes in which hole. This is where moves unravel.

The fix: One bag per component, not one bag per piece of furniture. A queen bed frame needs at minimum four separate labeled bags — headboard, footboard, side rails, and center support — not one bag labeled "bed." Take 90 extra seconds per piece. It saves 45 minutes of confusion at the other end.

Before you disassemble: photograph everything

Before loosening a single screw, photograph the assembled piece from multiple angles. For complex items — modular shelving systems, bed frames with multiple adjustment positions, desks with cable management — photograph the connection points specifically. These photos become your assembly guide at the other end and replace the instruction manual you can't find.

Store the photos in a clearly labeled album on your phone or back them up to cloud storage. We've seen people lose their photos when their phone dies on moving day — this is not the moment for that to happen.

Disassembly: the right technique for common furniture types

Bed frames and platform beds

Work from the top down: slats first, then center support beam, then side rails, then headboard and footboard last. Remove bolts and bag them per connection point. Lean panels against a wall with protective padding between them — never flat on the floor where they can be stepped on or damaged by boxes placed on top.

Bookshelves and modular shelving

Remove all contents before disassembly (obvious, but people forget). Cam locks are the most commonly stripped fastener in moves — use a properly fitted screwdriver, not a worn one, and never overtighten them during reassembly. If your shelving uses a back panel, it's almost always the last thing off and the first thing on.

Sectional sofas

Most sectionals connect via metal hooks or clips underneath — lift the section straight up, not at an angle, to disengage them cleanly. Mark each section with a piece of tape labeled "LEFT FACING" / "RIGHT FACING" / "CORNER" before disassembly so orientation is preserved at the other end. Reorienting a sectional incorrectly and then realizing it at 9pm is a morale-destroying end to moving day.

Dining tables

Table legs almost always attach with bolts through a bracket — remove the bolt, not the bracket. Keep the bracket attached to the table top and put the bolts in a labeled bag. The bracket is the alignment guide; if you remove it, reassembly becomes a guessing game.

Transport: how flat panels get damaged

Disassembled furniture panels are the most awkward items to load — long, flat, and easily set down wherever there's space, then forgotten under heavy boxes. The result: pressure cracks, veneer peeling, and corner dents that weren't there when the move started.

The correct loading approach for flat panels:

  • Transport vertically, never flat — vertical panels distribute weight along their strongest axis
  • Load last so they come off first and go directly to their destination room
  • Keep matching panels from the same piece together, not scattered around the truck
  • Use moving blankets between panels — wood-on-wood contact during transit causes finish damage
  • Never slide panels across the truck floor; lift and place them

Reassembly: the three rules that prevent most problems

Rule 1: Reverse the order of disassembly

Whatever came off last goes on first. For a bed frame: headboard and footboard first, then side rails, then center support, then slats. Working in the correct order ensures each component is structurally supported before the next one is added — which prevents racking, misalignment, and stripped holes from forcing pieces together out of sequence.

Rule 2: Hand-tighten everything before fully tightening anything

Get all fasteners started and finger-tight before using a tool on any of them. This lets the frame settle into its natural alignment. If you fully tighten one corner first, the frame locks into a slightly wrong position and every subsequent fastener fights against it. Hand-tighten all, check alignment, then go around with a tool.

Rule 3: Never force a piece that doesn't slide together smoothly

If a dowel, cam lock, or bolt isn't going in easily, stop. Forcing is how cam locks get stripped, how bolt holes get damaged, and how furniture goes from "needs assembly" to "needs replacing." Check the orientation, check whether you have the right component, and check whether there's a dowel misaligned beneath the surface. Ninety percent of the time, something is rotated 180 degrees.

Common mistakes that cause lasting damage

  • Over-tightening cam locks — they're designed to hold under light torque. Overtightening strips the cam housing, which is embedded in the panel and cannot be replaced easily.
  • Using the wrong size Allen key or screwdriver — slightly wrong sizes round bolt heads, making them impossible to remove or tighten in the future.
  • Skipping the alignment check — a bookshelf assembled 2 degrees off-square will rock on a flat floor indefinitely. Check square before fully tightening.
  • Assembling in the wrong room — a fully assembled king bed frame won't fit through most standard doorways. Assemble in the room it belongs in, not the nearest open space.

When to hand it off

Some furniture is genuinely difficult — not because it's poorly designed, but because it requires a second pair of hands to hold panels in alignment while fasteners are tightened, or because the tolerances are tight enough that experience with that manufacturer's hardware makes a real difference. If you've tried the steps above and are still staring at a pile of panels an hour later, that's what professional furniture assembly is for.
