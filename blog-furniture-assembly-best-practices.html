Furniture Assembly Guide

Furniture Assembly Best Practices

By the Rest Assured Moving crew · Salem, OR

Almost every furniture assembly problem we get called to fix traces back to one of three moments: disassembly, transport, or reassembly. Here's where things typically go wrong — and the system that prevents each one.

Where disassembly goes wrong

The most common mistake is removing hardware without tracking which piece it came from. Screws, bolts, and cam locks for a bed frame look interchangeable until you're holding six identical-looking bags two weeks later with no idea which one fits the headboard.

The fix: bag hardware per component, not per furniture piece. A bed frame might need four separate labeled bags — headboard, footboard, side rails, slats — rather than one bag for "the bed."

Where transport goes wrong

Disassembled furniture parts are often the most awkward items to move — long, flat panels that get set down wherever there's space, then forgotten or damaged under heavier boxes.

The fix: transport flat panels vertically, never flat, and load them last so they come off first. Keep matching pieces from the same furniture item together rather than scattered across the truck wherever space allowed.

Where reassembly goes wrong

This is where most DIY furniture assembly actually falls apart — literally. Stripped screw holes from over-tightening, parts forced together in the wrong order, or hardware bags that never made it off the truck are the top three issues we see.

The fix: reassemble in the reverse order of disassembly, hand-tighten every fastener before fully tightening any of them (this lets the frame settle into alignment), and never force a piece that doesn't slide together easily — it usually means a part is oriented wrong.

Furniture that benefits most from professional assembly

  • Bed frames and bunk beds — multiple panels, structural bolts, and weight-bearing joints
  • Sectionals — modular connectors that are easy to misalign
  • Bookshelves and modular shelving — cam locks and dowels that strip easily if forced
  • Dining tables and desks — leg brackets that need to be torqued evenly to avoid wobble

A simple rule worth following

If you're disassembling something to move it, photograph each step before removing a part. It takes thirty extra seconds and turns reassembly into matching photos rather than guessing.
