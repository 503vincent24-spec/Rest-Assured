Packing Guide

How to Pack Fragile Items Like a Professional

By the Rest Assured Moving crew — Salem, Oregon

Improper packing is the single leading cause of move-related damage. Most breakage isn't from careless loading — it's from items that weren't secured tightly enough in the box to begin with. Here's the exact technique our crew uses, and what we've seen go wrong when people cut corners.

Start with the right materials — not whatever's on hand

Before anything gets wrapped, gather the correct supplies. Using newspaper instead of packing paper, undersized boxes, or single-layer cardboard for heavy items are the shortcuts that lead to broken belongings.

  • Dish-pack boxes — double-walled, purpose-built for plates, bowls, and mugs. Regular boxes compress and fail under stack weight.
  • Unprinted packing paper — not newspaper. Ink transfers onto dishware and is difficult to remove from white dishes and ceramics.
  • Bubble wrap — for curved or high-value items: stemware, decorative ceramics, anything with a delicate profile.
  • Adjustable picture/mirror boxes — telescoping sides that fit the exact dimensions of your piece. Non-negotiable for anything over 18 inches.
  • Cardboard corner protectors — for framed artwork, large mirrors, and glass-topped furniture.
  • Quality packing tape and a tape gun — masking tape and scotch tape fail under box weight. Use 2-inch packing tape only.

Dishes and plates — the edge-stacking rule

The most common packing mistake we see: plates stacked flat, one on top of another, like pancakes. Flat-stacked plates act as a single unit and crack from the bottom up under the weight of boxes above them. The correct technique:

  1. Line the bottom of a dish-pack box with 3–4 inches of crumpled packing paper.
  2. Wrap each plate individually — center the plate on a sheet of paper, fold in the corners, roll to close.
  3. Stand plates on edge in the box, like records in a crate. Edge-stacked plates distribute impact laterally rather than compressing vertically.
  4. Fill all side gaps with crumpled paper — there should be zero movement when you shake the sealed box.
  5. Add another 3-inch paper layer on top before closing.

Glassware and stemware

Glasses and wine glasses are the most commonly broken items in a move. The stem of a wine glass is nearly impossible to protect in transit without the right technique:

  1. Stuff the inside of every glass with crumpled packing paper first — this cushions from the inside out.
  2. Wrap the outside individually in bubble wrap, securing with tape.
  3. Pack glasses standing upright (never upside down) in a dish-pack box.
  4. For stemware, wrap the stem and bowl separately with extra bubble wrap before the final wrap.
  5. Pack stems toward the center of the box, away from the walls.
  6. Label every side: "FRAGILE — GLASS — THIS SIDE UP."

Mirrors, framed art, and glass panels

Mirrors and framed artwork require a picture/mirror box and corner protectors — no exceptions for anything you care about. The technique:

  1. Tape an X across the glass with painter's tape. This doesn't prevent breaking, but it holds the shards together if it does break in transit.
  2. Apply corner protectors to all four corners.
  3. Wrap the full piece in bubble wrap, secured with tape that doesn't touch the frame finish directly.
  4. Box it using an adjustable picture box sized to the piece.
  5. Always transport mirrors and large frames vertically — never flat. Flat transport creates pressure stress on the glass that vertical transport doesn't.

Electronics and screens

Original manufacturer boxes are the gold standard for electronics — if you still have them, use them. If not:

  1. For screens (TVs, monitors): wrap in an anti-static layer first (a clean microfiber cloth or the anti-static bag it came in), then bubble wrap. Never let packing tape touch a screen surface directly.
  2. Photograph every cable connection before disconnecting anything.
  3. Bag all cables and remotes and label them with the device they belong to.
  4. Pack electronics in snug-fitting boxes — too much empty space means movement, which means damage.
  5. Mark boxes "FRAGILE — ELECTRONICS — THIS SIDE UP" and do not stack heavy boxes on top.

Lamps and lampshades

Lampshades are routinely damaged because people pack them wrong: crushed into a box under pressure, or stacked with other items that dent the shade. The correct approach:

  1. Remove the bulb and pack it separately — bulbs packed with the lamp get broken every time.
  2. Wrap the lamp base in bubble wrap.
  3. Pack the shade in its own box with loose crumpled paper inside the shade and around it — never compressed against it. Most shade damage is permanent denting from compression.
  4. Never pack a lampshade in the same box as anything heavy.

The one rule that prevents most breakage

Pick up any sealed box. Shake it gently. If you can hear or feel movement inside — the box isn't ready to load. Every fragile item should be packed tightly enough that nothing shifts, with cushioning filling every gap. If there's movement, add more crumpled paper until there isn't.

This single test, applied to every fragile box before it's loaded, would prevent the majority of move-related breakage we've ever seen.
